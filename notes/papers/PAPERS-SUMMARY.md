## B. Local PDFs (`notes/papers/`)

### B1. Fraxanet 2025/2026: DerStandard dataset paper (arXiv v2, 21 pp.). Coverage: full text.
- **What:** data descriptor of the dataset we use. DerStandard.at, 2013-2022, scraped by plain HTTP; all persistent IDs hashed (BLAKE2s, 8-byte digest, secret salt); article IDs not anonymized; **no comment text** released (only 896-dim KaLM-embedding-multilingual-mini-v1 embeddings, CPU-computed).
- **Table 1 numbers:** 586,942 articles (25 Topic_1, 305 Topic_2, 1,722 Topic_3 labels); 75,644,850 comments (21,957,527 threads; 53,617,134 replies = 71%); 412,511,165 votes (77% positive = 319,154,131); 247,863 users (52% voted >10 times, 36% commented >10 times, **44% active ≥1 year, 0.45% active all 10 years**).
- **Files/fields (Fig. 4):** Users (User_Id, N_Comments, First/Last_Comment, R_Pos/R_Neg received, G_Pos/G_Neg given, First/Last_Vote); Comments (Comment_Id, Author_Id, Is_Reply, Parent_Id, Thread_level, Origin_Parent_Id, Timestamp, Article_Id, Votes_Pos/Neg); Votes (Comment_Id, Voter_Id, Author_Id, Pos, Neg); Articles (Article_Id, Topic_1-3, Timestamp...).
- **Data-quality facts (section Internal Consistency), important for our cleaning:**
  - Missing authors (deleted users): comments 8.46% (SD 3.78) of authors NA; votes: 8.33% author NA, 7.27% voter NA.
  - ~1,000 article IDs in comments missing from the articles file: 382,167 comments (0.5%) flagged "Article deleted".
  - Vote count in votes files is sometimes one higher than in comments files; **the authors replaced the comments-file vote counts with the aggregates from the votes files**.
  - **1,208 comments have a timestamp earlier than their article**: "may affect lifetime estimates of <0.15% of the 247,863 users"; flagged as "Inconsistent timestamp" in `inconsistent_comment_metadata`. They recommend excluding affected rows when estimating lifespans.
- **Factions:** `faction_users` = 14,817 users (≈62% in the larger faction), from FAULTANA, **static** (long-term alignment 2014-2021; no shifts over time).
- **Prior uses of the data:** COVID sentiment dashboard (Pellert 2020), validation of sentiment vs survey mood (Pellert 2022), FAULTANA.
- **Link to our work:** our funnel (duplicates, deleted voters, inconsistent IDs) matches their warnings. **Open point:** do we exclude "Inconsistent timestamp" comments when computing lifetime? (checked in the code review, see `CODE-REVIEW.md`).

### B2. Fraxanet et al. 2024: FAULTANA (PNAS Nexus; 12 pp. + SI 19 pp.). Coverage: full main text and SI text; figures not inspected.
- **Idea:** from signed interaction data find the main fault line (optimal 2-group partition minimizing frustrated edges), then measure two separable things: **Antagonism** (share of negative interactions) and **Alignment** (how well interactions follow the fault line). Product = polarization. Also **Cohesiveness** (positive share of within-group edges) and **Divisiveness** (negative share of between-group edges), renormalized by subtracting a null (sign-shuffled) value.
- **Network construction (what we inherited):** each pair (i,j) has p_ij ~ Bernoulli with Beta(α0, β0) prior updated by counts; posterior mean E[p]=α/(α+β), Var = αβ/((α+β)²(α+β+1)). **Edge defined only if E[p] > 0.6 (positive) or < 0.4 (negative) and Var < 1e-4.** Birdwatch: uniform prior. **DerStandard: α=1, β=2** ("slightly favours negative" because negative votes are under-represented and carry a stronger signal).
- **DerStandard network:** stable users = rated at least once per year 2014-2021 → ~14.8k users (SI table: 14,827), ~76M pairwise interactions, 5.56M edges, **41% negative edges; 17% negative interactions** (our whole-dataset negative vote share is ~23%). k*=2 groups; largest = 62% of nodes; SAI_G = 0.3955 [0.3951, 0.3961]; frustrated edges 29%; Cohesiveness 0.70 (norm. 0.14), Divisiveness 0.73 (norm. 0.29). Partition by approximate algorithm (200 runs, keep best).
- **Findings:** topics with high antagonism but low alignment: refugees, COVID politics; high alignment: national elections, abortion, corruption allegations. Alignment and Antagonism uncorrelated across topics (r = -0.0016); Cohesiveness and Divisiveness correlated (r = 0.83). Alignment rose from 2016 (migration crisis, Cologne 2015/16) to 2017, dropped after elections.
- **Limitations stated by authors:** approximate partitions; fixed membership over time; frustration index only informative when groups are few and of similar size.
- **Link to our work:** the source of our H3 factions, of the Beta prior and of the 0.6/0.4 thresholds. **Their edges use the whole 8 years of votes, so factions and our dyad stance are built from the same votes → partial circularity in H3b** (our NOTES).

### B3. Lan Ceqin 2026: MSc thesis, UPF (supervisors Kaltenbrunner, Gómez), "Conversational and Evaluative Reciprocity in Online News Discussions: A Longitudinal Network Analysis of DerStandard, 2013-2022" (110 pp.). Coverage: chapters 1-2 (methods) in full, 3.1-3.2, 3.4.1-3.4.3, 3.7, chapter 4 in full; sections 3.3, 3.4.4-3.6 and the appendix (generative thread model) only skimmed.
- **Design:** weekly directed networks (reply; total/positive/negative voting; intersection of reply+vote), also by week × Topic_1 category (top 7: panorama, inland, international, diskurs, wirtschaft, web, sport). Votes are assigned to the **week of the voted comment** (no vote timestamps; same limitation as ours). Reciprocity r = |{(i,j)∈E : (j,i)∈E}| / |E|. Self-loops removed.
- **Findings:** reply reciprocity stable ~0.33-0.35 until 2020, then jumps to >0.44 around **2020-W09** (first Austrian COVID cases), stays high through 2021, declines in 2022. Voting reciprocity smoother. "diskurs" consistently most reciprocal. Permutation test (5,000 perms): post−pre difference 0.066 far outside null. Reply vs voting reciprocity correlation r=0.338 (Pearson), 0.365 (Spearman), N=9,150 week-category bins.
- **Context facts useful for us:** new-user share highest at the start (artefact of window start), then low; **weekly comment volume rises 2013→2021 and falls sharply in 2022**, consistent with our observation of a ~45% drop from Feb 2022. Generative thread model (appendix): reply-back parameter rises 3.487 (pre) → 4.759 (COVID) → 4.260 (post).
- **Authors' limitations/future work:** no vote timestamps; no text; observational; future work = combine with text/stance, user-level dynamics.
- **Link to our work:** cited as prior work on reply/vote reciprocity; **our reply-back analysis (P(B replies | A downvoted) 31.8% vs 16.6%) is a signed, comment-level counterpart of her reciprocity**. Our novelty = stance × replies × lifetime.

### B4. Steegen et al. 2016: Multiverse analysis (Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(5), 702-712). Coverage: full.
- **Claim:** data are "constructed" (exclusions, coding, cut-offs); each defensible combination gives a data set → a **multiverse** of results. Report all, show which choices matter. Example: fertility × religiosity (Durante et al. 2013): 5×2-3×3×3×2 combinations; 120 (Study 1) / 210 (Study 2) data sets after dropping inconsistent combinations.
- **Points to remember:** only *reasonable* options; inconsistent combinations are removed; it is **not a formal test and gives no single summary** (suggests averaging p values as one option); preregistration does not remove arbitrariness; "deflating the multiverse" needs better theory/measurement.
- **Link to our work (`07_multiverse.py`):** our "multiverse-lite" (24 H2 specs, 18 H4 specs) is Steegen-style **descriptive** robustness over data-processing and modelling choices; it includes no joint inference.

### B5. Simonsohn, Simmons, Nelson 2020: Specification curve analysis (Nat Hum Behav 4, 1208-1214; SI 25 pp.). Coverage: main text full; SI sections 1-ish (hurricane specification set) read, rest skimmed.
- **Three steps:** (1) define the set of **theoretically justified, statistically valid, non-redundant** specifications; (2) plot the **descriptive curve** (sorted estimates + "dashboard" of choices); (3) **joint inference** by resampling under the null (shuffle the key predictor for experiments; for non-experimental data impose the null by y* = y − b̂x and bootstrap rows, same rows for all K specs). Test statistics: median effect, share significant in the predicted direction, aggregate Stouffer Z.
- **Warnings:** specifications must all be valid tests of the question (not blind robustness); specification curve reduces but does not eliminate subjectivity; equal weights by default.
- **Link to our work:** we have step 1-2 analogues only. **We do not do step 3**, so we cannot say "the whole curve rejects the null" in their formal sense; with N in the millions the expected curve under the null is flat at ~0, so the descriptive result is already informative, but a careful thesis should say so.

### B6. Heider 1946: Attitudes and cognitive organization (J. Psychology 21, 107-112; 4 pp.). Coverage: full.
- p likes (L) / dislikes o or x; unit relations (U: similarity, causality, ownership). **Balanced** if all parts of a unit have the same dynamic character; for three entities: all positive or **two negative and one positive**; imbalance → tension and forces toward balance. L is logically asymmetric but psychologically tends to be symmetric (pLo ↔ oLp) and transitive.
- **Link:** theoretical root of "friend of a friend / enemy of an enemy"; justifies why antagonism and alignment structure may be two camps.

### B7. Cartwright & Harary 1956: Structural balance: a generalization of Heider's theory (Psychol Rev 63(5), 277-293). Coverage: full.
- Formalizes balance with signed (di)graphs. A signed graph is **balanced iff every cycle is positive** (cycle sign = product of edge signs) ⇔ **all paths between two points have the same sign** ⇔ **structure theorem: the nodes split into two sets, positive edges inside, negative between** (one set may be empty). Extends to digraphs (semicycles), more than 3 entities, different relation types.
- **Degree of balance** b(G) = positive cycles / all cycles (probability a random cycle is positive); N-balance (only cycles up to length N); local balance at a node.
- Distinguishes "complement" (not liking) from "opposite" (disliking); **absence of a tie ≠ negative tie** (relevant to us: no vote ≠ downvote).
- **Link:** formal basis of FAULTANA's two-faction partition (frustrated edge = edge violating the structure theorem) and of Aref & Wilson's partial balance.

### B8. D'Ignazi, Kaltenbrunner, Le Mens, Germano, Gómez 2025: Rewarding engagement and personalization in popularity-based rankings amplifies extremism and polarization (arXiv 2510.24354). Coverage: introduction, model, methods and results/discussion read; appendices not read.
- Model of user–ranking feedback with 5 stances (−2..2): position bias (click prob ∝ ρ^−rank, estimated ρ̂=1.09), stance-aligned clicking (centre users ~2× baseline, extremes ~1.5×), U-shaped active engagement (extremes engage ~47-50%, moderates 30-40%). Simulations + Prolific experiments (432 static, 1,534 dynamic participants, US). Personalization + engagement reward raises extremism and polarization (~20% more clicks to extreme same-stance content; 13% more same-stance clicks overall).
- **Link:** none to our pipeline. One conceptual hook: extreme users engage more, so the most active (and most hostile) accounts may be a polarized minority, a possible confound for hostility measures. Same research group.

### B9. Amarasinghe, Romano, Amidei, Vincent, Kaltenbrunner 2026: Uncertainty-aware estimation of mis/disinformation prevalence (arXiv 2603.11058). Coverage: abstract, introduction, background and methods read; results not read.
- Quantifies three uncertainty sources of a prevalence estimate: sampling (**Wilson score interval**), annotation (**multinomial simulation** using a 3×3 junior→senior transition matrix from a double-coded 20% subset, S=500), data retrieval (**two-level bootstrap** over keywords then posts, 500×500; joint version 100³). Data: six platforms, four EU countries, March-April 2025, view-weighted sample of 3,000 posts per language.
- **Link:** none to our data. Methodologically relevant only as an example of uncertainty decomposition in this group; Wilson interval is the recommended alternative to Wald for proportions at small n (irrelevant at our N).

## C. Cross-cutting facts and flags for the TFG (derived from the above)

1. **Timestamp inconsistency (dataset paper):** 1,208 comments precede their article; affect <0.15% of users' lifetimes. Check that the lifetime code handles it (see code review).
2. **Vote counts:** comments-file `votes_pos/neg` can differ from the votes files by one; the authors overwrote them with the votes-file aggregates. Our H4 uses `comments.parquet` `votes_pos/votes_neg` for received votes, and `votes_clean` elsewhere (check consistency in the code review).
3. **Faction file size:** dataset paper says 14,817 users; FAULTANA SI table says 14,827 for the 8-year network. Our PLAN uses 14,817 (9,278 + 5,539). Minor, but cite the dataset paper.
4. **FAULTANA negative share:** 17% of interactions for the 14.8k stable users vs ~23% in the whole dataset. Our H3 restricts to those users, so stance levels for them are not comparable to platform-wide levels.
5. **Multiverse/spec curve:** our robustness is descriptive. Say "robustness across 24/18 specifications", not "inferentially robust".
6. **2022 volume drop:** Lan 2026 also reports a sharp comment-volume decline in 2022 → supports a real platform/data feature, not a pipeline artefact. Still unexplained.
7. **No-vote ≠ downvote** (Cartwright & Harary's complement vs opposite): in H4 "hostile reply" requires an observed downvote; replies where A did not vote are neutral/unknown (~75% of replies).
8. **Novelty claim:** FAULTANA (antagonism/alignment, factions) and Lan (reciprocity) do not combine reply structure × signed stance × lifetime; neither studies user lifetime.

### D1. Cheng, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil, Leskovec 2014: How Community Feedback Shapes User Behavior (ICWSM 2014; arXiv 1405.1429). File: Cheng2014_community-feedback-user-behavior.pdf. Coverage: full text (figures as text only).
- **Data:** CNN, Breitbart, IGN, Allkpop; timestamped comments and votes, Mar 2012-Aug 2013 (~42M comments, ~140M votes, 1.8M users); only users who joined after Mar 2012 (so their whole life is observed). Proportion of up-votes: 0.82-0.95 per community.
- **Measure:** feedback = proportion of up-votes p=P/(P+N), validated with a Mechanical Turk experiment (R2 0.92 vs 0.88 for P-N, 0.41 for P). "Negative evaluation" = post in lowest quartile of p (>=10 votes), "positive" = top quartile.
- **Method:** propensity-style matching of users A (positive) and B (negative) with equal *textual* quality (predicted from bigrams), equal past activity and past feedback; then compare the next 3 posts. Residual p - q (true minus text-predicted up-vote share) = "community bias".
- **Findings:** (1) negatively evaluated users write *worse* subsequent posts; positive feedback has no significant effect. (2) Community perception: later posts by punished users are judged worse than their text deserves. (3) **Negative feedback increases posting frequency (~+30%), positive +20%, no feedback -15%**. (4) **Retention: users who receive no feedback are the most likely to leave; punished users stay slightly more than rewarded ones.** (5) "Tit-for-tat": share of up-votes given correlates linearly with share received; negatively evaluated users downvote others more in the following week. (6) Voting networks around a post: fraction of balanced triangles peaks when votes are split 50/50 (structural balance, citing Cartwright & Harary).
- **Flags / link to our TFG:**
  - **Tension with our H4.** We find early downvote share received goes with *leaving sooner* (HR 1.11) and "got any reply" with leaving later (HR 0.95); they find negative feedback keeps people posting and *no feedback* is what predicts leaving. The two are compatible only if the outcomes are read carefully: they measure activity/retention in the next posts after one evaluation, we measure time to the last activity from day 30 using early-share exposure. Say this explicitly in the thesis; it is the best literature anchor for H4. Also their "no feedback -> leave" resembles our got_any_reply result.
  - Their tit-for-tat finding is a literature precedent for our reply-back table (P(B replies | A downvoted)=31.8%) and for "hostile replies written".
  - Same limitation family: their votes are timestamped, ours are not (month only).
  - Methodologically they use matching on text quality, which we cannot do (no text).

### D2. Cheng, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil, Leskovec 2015: Antisocial Behavior in Online Discussion Communities (ICWSM 2015; arXiv 1504.00680). File: Cheng2015_antisocial-behavior.pdf. Coverage: abstract, introduction, related work, data and measurement sections, and the longitudinal-analysis section; the typology and prediction sections only via the introduction's summary.
- **Data:** CNN, Breitbart, IGN (Disqus), Mar 2012-Aug 2013 (~40M posts, >100M votes, 1.7M users); ground truth = users **permanently banned by moderators** (Future-Banned Users, FBUs: N=10,476 CNN, 660 IGN, 736 Breitbart, after filtering) vs matched Never-Banned Users (matched on number of posts and posts per day).
- **Why they do not use down-votes as the measure of bad behaviour (quote-worthy):** "down-voting may signal disagreement rather than undesirability", and Breitbart has only 4% down-votes. They use deletions and bans instead. **This is a direct caveat for our C1 (votes as stance proxy): a downvote can mean disagreement OR disapproval of quality.**
- **Findings:** FBUs write less like others in their thread, are harder to read, less positive, concentrate on few threads, receive more replies (they "lure" replies). Their post quality worsens over time and the community becomes less tolerant (deletion rate rises even after controlling for text quality). **Users whose first posts are excessively deleted are more likely to write worse later** (matched comparison). Bimodal deletion rates among FBUs -> typology (getting worse vs. redeeming). Banning predicted with >80% AUC from 5-10 posts.
- **Methods used (for reference):** matching (Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983), Mechanical Turk text-quality ratings (Krippendorff alpha 0.35-0.39, weak), bigram logistic regression (AUC 0.70).
- **Link / flags for our TFG:**
  - Our H-1 limitation (deleted users invisible) is exactly this group: banned users' exits are not observable in DerStandard. Cheng's FBUs show that the users most associated with hostility are also a distinct population; our H4 averages over them.
  - Supports reading "hostile replies written" as a user-level trait, which is the interpretation risk in our long-lived analysis.
  - Not reproducible in our data (no text, no deletions/bans), so cite as framing only.

### D3. Leskovec, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg 2010: Predicting Positive and Negative Links in Online Social Networks (WWW 2010; arXiv 1003.2429). File: Leskovec2010_predicting-signed-links.pdf. Coverage: abstract, introduction, data, prediction section and balance/status comparison; global-structure section only via the introduction.
- **Task:** *edge sign prediction*: hide sign s(u,v), infer it from the other signs around the nodes. Datasets with explicit signs: **Epinions** (trust/distrust; 119,217 nodes, 841k edges, 85.0% positive), **Slashdot** (friend/foe, "Slashdot Zoo"), **Wikipedia** admin-election votes (for/against). In all three about 80% of edges are positive; they also use balanced samples (random guess = 50%).
- **Method:** logistic regression on degree features and on triad (2-path) features, 10-fold cross-validation, restricted to edges with a minimum embeddedness (default 25). Models generalise across domains with small loss when trained on one and tested on another; Wikipedia is hardest (public, consequential votes).
- **Main idea for us:** compare the learned model with **structural balance** ("enemy of my friend is my enemy") and **status theory** (positive link = u thinks v has higher status). Balance and status agree on some triads and disagree on others; balance fails mainly where two negative edges are predicted to give a positive one ("enemy of my enemy" is not reliably a friend). Negative links also improve prediction of positive links (up to ~1.5x over random).
- **Link to our TFG:**
  - Gives the vocabulary and prior art for *signed edges built from votes* (our H2 edges) and for evaluating them.
  - **Caution for H3:** two-camp balance (Cartwright-Harary, FAULTANA) is not the only plausible structure; status theory predicts directed, asymmetric patterns, which would show up as A->B and B->A having different signs (relevant to the "both directions of each pair" analysis we designed).
  - Their signs are explicit relations (trust, friend, vote for admin), ours are inferred from vote counts through a Beta rule, which is a weaker, noisier definition of "link sign". State that distinction in the methods chapter.

### D4. Leskovec, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg 2010: Signed Networks in Social Media (CHI 2010; arXiv 1003.2424). File: Leskovec2010_signed-networks-social-media.pdf. Coverage: abstract, introduction and the balance-vs-status comparison; later sections not read.
- Companion paper to D3, aimed at a social-computing audience. Evaluates **structural balance** vs **status theory** on the same three sites by counting all signed triad types (directed/undirected, respecting or ignoring the order in which edges formed).
- **Conclusion:** balance explains certain common patterns (e.g. all-positive triads over-represented), but **status theory better explains local patterns of signed links in these directed, evolving networks**, and extends to heterogeneity in users' linking tendencies (generative vs receptive baselines: some users give mostly positive signs, others receive them).
- **Link to our TFG:** supports being careful with "two camps" as the only reading of antagonism. Also the idea of **generative baseline (how positive A usually is)** vs **receptive baseline (how positive others are toward B)** is exactly the confound in our H2/H4 hostility measures: a replier who downvotes everyone looks hostile toward B. Our null model (random co-present target) partly handles the generative baseline of A.

### D5. Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil, West, Jurafsky, Leskovec, Potts 2013: No Country for Old Members (WWW 2013, best paper; pp. 307-318). File: DanescuNiculescuMizil2013_no-country-old-members.pdf (author copy from Cornell). Coverage: abstract, introduction, data/methods, lifecycle section (user-level change), start of prediction section; feature details and results tables not read.
- **Data:** BeerAdvocate and RateBeer (beer reviews, ~10 years, SNAP data). Text-based: language models per month, cross-entropy of a user's posts against the community's snapshot model.
- **Findings:** users follow a two-stage lifecycle: an *innovative learning phase* (increasing receptivity to community language up to about one third of their eventual lifespan, "linguistic adolescence"), then a *conservative phase* in which the user stops following community change until abandonment. The pattern is "elastic": it stretches with eventual lifespan. Users with longer eventual lifespan have higher receptivity throughout. Early language alone (first few reviews) predicts total lifetime.
- **How they define lifetime (useful to compare with ours):** joined = time of first post; **abandoned = no post for at least one year**; for full-lifespan analyses they **drop users who post on or after Jan 2011** (the last month) to enforce the one-year rule. We instead define lifetime = last-first activity (comments or votes) and treat last activity after 2022-07-01 (a 6-month buffer; the multiverse tries 3/6/12 months) as right-censored, keeping those users in the survival analysis. Their approach discards censored users; ours uses KM/Cox. Mention both in the thesis methods.
- **Link to our TFG:** (1) frames lifetime/churn as an outcome predictable from early behaviour: our H4 asks the same with votes and replies instead of language. (2) Their finding that early signals predict lifetime supports a landmark/early-window design. (3) Text-based, so not reproducible with our data (no text). (4) Flag for the "long-lived users" analysis: they note lifespan is a function of the post count; users with more posts are not comparable with users with few unless activity is controlled, which is why we plan activity strata.

### D6. Gómez, Kaltenbrunner, López 2008: Statistical Analysis of the Social Network and Discussion Threads in Slashdot (WWW 2008, pp. 645-654). File: Gomez2008_slashdot-network-threads.pdf (conference archive). Coverage: abstract, introduction, network-construction and general-properties sections, start of the thread-structure section; the radial-tree and controversy-measure details not read in full.
- **Data:** Slashdot (tech news with a -1..+5 moderation score per comment); users with >=10 comments (18,476 users).
- **Networks built from comments:** (1) users linked if one replies to the other (directed); (2) bidirectional links only; (3) undirected sparse graph of *reciprocal* reply exchanges. Findings: giant component, short paths, high clustering, **only moderate reciprocity**, neutral degree assortativity; degree distributions fit **log-normal** better than power-law (Kolmogorov-Smirnov).
- **Threads:** radial tree representation; heterogeneity and self-similarity across nesting levels; a simple **controversy measure** per post (see the paper's section 3 for the definition).
- **Link to our TFG:** the oldest paper in the supervisor's group on reply networks: the template of "reply -> directed edge between users" (our C2) and of reciprocity (our reply-back table; later Lan Ceqin's thesis). Their controversy measure is a candidate depth-based comparison for our depth breakdown.

### D7. Gómez, Kappen, Litvak, Kaltenbrunner 2013: A likelihood-based framework for the analysis of discussion threads (World Wide Web 16(5), 645-675; arXiv 1203.0652; open access). ### D9. Wang, Peduzzi, Wininger, Ma 2022: Statistical Methods for Accommodating Immortal Time: A Selective Review and Comparison (arXiv 2202.02369). File: Wang2022_immortal-time-methods-review.pdf. ### D11. Easley & Kleinberg 2010: Networks, Crowds, and Markets, Chapter 5 "Positive and Negative Relationships" (Cambridge UP; free preprint). File: EasleyKleinberg2010_ch5-positive-negative-relationships.pdf (chapter 5 only, from the authors' site). Coverage: section headings, Balance Theorem, weak balance, trust/distrust discussion and the statement of the approximate-balance claims; the proofs were skimmed.
- **Structural balance** for complete signed graphs: every triangle must have 3 positive edges or 1 positive + 2 negative. **Balance Theorem (Harary 1953):** a balanced complete graph is either all friends or splits into two groups X and Y, friends within, enemies between. Equivalent graph version (Cartwright-Harary): balanced iff no cycle with an odd number of negative edges.
- **Weak structural balance (section 5.4):** relax the rule by allowing the "three negative edges" triangle (enemy of my enemy need not be a friend). Then the graph divides into **any number of groups**, friends inside and enemies between. Motivation: Cartwright-Harary predict only two camps or consensus.
- **Approximate balance (section 5.5):** if at least 99.9% of triangles are balanced, then either almost everyone is friends or the nodes split into two groups with almost all within-group pairs friendly and almost all between-group pairs enemies (the claim is stated with an epsilon bound; proof by neighbourhood arguments).
- **Trust/distrust vs friend/enemy:** edges are directed and the meaning of "distrust" differs: "enemy of my enemy is my friend" (balance) vs "distrust means the other is worse than me" (status). Same point as Leskovec et al. (D3, D4).
- **Link to our TFG:** the clearest short textbook treatment of what our H3 assumes. **Flag:** FAULTANA forces two groups (k*=2) and the frustration index is only informative when groups are few and of similar size (see B2). If the data support *weak* balance (several factions), a two-faction analysis can overstate antagonism within the minority side. Worth one sentence in the limitations, and a possible check (k>2 partitions).

### D12. Aref & Wilson 2018: Measuring Partial Balance in Signed Networks (Journal of Complex Networks 6(4), 566-595; arXiv 1509.04037v6). File: ArefWilson2018_partial-balance-measures.pdf. ### E2. Simonsohn, Simmons, Nelson 2020 (B5), now also: joint-inference section of the main text and SI Supplements 4-5. Still not read: SI Supplements 1-3 beyond the hurricane example, the resume example details.
- **Three joint test statistics** (null: the effect of x on y is zero in all specifications): (1) the **median effect** across specifications, (2) the **share of specifications significant in the predicted direction**, (3) the **average Z (Stouffer)** of all specification p-values, weighted 1/sqrt(K). Recommended: report (2) for intuition and (3) for efficiency. No analytic null exists because specifications are not independent, so the null distribution is obtained by **resampling under the null**.
- **Experiments:** shuffle the randomised variable (exchangeability), re-estimate all K specifications on each shuffled dataset (500 shuffles in their examples). P = share of shuffled curves at least as extreme (halved for the two-sided "dominant sign" version; reported as P<0.002 if none of 500 is as extreme).
- **Observational data (six steps):** (1) estimate all K specifications on the real data, obtaining b_k; (2) force the null with K new outcomes y*_k = y_k - b_k x_k; (3) draw N rows with replacement, **the same drawn rows for all K specifications**; (4) re-estimate the K specifications on the draw; (5) repeat 500-1,000 times; (6) compare the observed statistic with the bootstrap distribution. The bootstrap also corrects specifications with inflated false-positive rates (SI Supplement 4: five Poisson regressions with >40% false positives still give ~5% jointly; the Stouffer-type test has more power than any single specification).
- **Mapping to our TFG (not done yet):**
  - **H2 (24 specs):** our natural null is the **paired label swap** (within each reply edge, swap which target is called real and which null) or, equivalently, the bootstrap-under-null above with y*_k = y_k - b_k x_k; resampling unit = replier (as in our current bootstrap), identical rows across all 24 specifications.
  - **H4 (18 specs):** force the null by removing each specification's estimated effect from the linear predictor (Cox: permute the exposure across users within cohort and landmark-eligible group) and resample users; same drawn users across specifications.
  - **Cost note:** 500 resamples x 24 (or 18) specs; each H2 spec is a few seconds on the edge-level table, each H4 spec is a Cox fit; expect tens of minutes, not hours. Not run.
- **Visual conventions to copy:** sorted curve with the bootstrap null band (2.5/50/97.5 percentiles) and a lower panel marking which analytic choices each specification used; stacked bars of the share of operationalisations per estimate range (SI Supplement 5). Our `07_multiverse.png` has only the upper panel.

### E5. Gomez et al. 2013 (D7), now also: model section, estimation, validation, empirical results. Not read: appendix derivations, sections 4.4-4.5 beyond headings.
- **Model details:** a thread is a growing tree; at step t the new comment attaches to existing node k with probability p(parent=k | history) = A(k)/Z_t. **Time is comment arrival order, not wall-clock** (this alleviates heterogeneity of rhythms). Attractiveness A(k) combines **popularity** (degree d_k, weight beta, linear preferential attachment; starts at 1 for every node), **novelty** (tau^(t-k+1), exponentially decaying with tau in [0,1], so tau near 1 means very slow decay) and a **root bias** (extra initial popularity at the root only).
- **Estimation:** full likelihood over all node arrivals in all threads (threads assumed i.i.d.), numerically maximised; checked on synthetic threads (unbiased; a few hundred threads suffice; five random initialisations to look for local minima). Nested models (full, no popularity, no novelty, no root bias) are compared with **likelihood-ratio tests** and with ANOVA + Tukey's range test on the negative log-likelihoods across samples.
- **Findings:** the full model is preferred on all datasets except Wikipedia (not distinguishable from the no-popularity model). **Slashdot:** popularity is the most relevant feature, novelty decays very slowly (tau close to 1). **Barrapunto:** novelty most relevant. **Meneame:** the distinction between replying to the post and to a comment (root bias) matters most, then novelty. **Wikipedia:** novelty more relevant than root bias; beta = 0. Thread-size distribution: log-normal-like for Slashdot, power-law-like tail for Meneame (> 50 comments); repeated participation by the same user is typical. Synthetic threads from the full model reproduce degree distributions, subtree sizes and depth vs size.
- **Implication for our null:** the model uses only the thread structure (no authors, no votes), so it gives *comment-level* attraction probabilities; to use it as a null for H2 we would fit (beta, tau, root bias) on DerStandard threads per period and draw the null target comment from A(k), then take its author. Cost: one MLE per period on a sample of threads (as in Lan Ceqin's 684 per period), not on 20M threads. Cheap compared with the dyad pipeline. Caveat: it ignores *topic* and *who* is posting, so it is a structural, not a user-level, baseline.

### E7. Leskovec, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg 2010, WWW (D3), now also: Section 4 (global structure), Section 5 (positive-edge prediction) heading and Section 6 (conclusion).
